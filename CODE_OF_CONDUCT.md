# Code of Conduct

## The short version

Be decent to people. Criticise work, not the person who did it. Assume the
other party is acting in good faith until they show you otherwise.

This is a small project maintained by one person in their spare time. It does
not need elaborate governance; it needs everyone involved to behave like an
adult.

## What is expected

**Technical criticism is welcome and wanted.** This project asks people to poke
holes in it — that is the point of publishing it. "This design is wrong and
here is why" is a contribution, not an attack, and it will not be treated as
one.

**Say what you mean.** Blunt is fine. Personal is not. The difference is
whether the sentence is about the code or about the human.

**Assume competence.** People arrive with different backgrounds. Someone who
does not know a DNS internal is not stupid; someone who disagrees with you is
not acting in bad faith.

**Accept that the answer may be no.** Scope discipline is what keeps this
project usable. A rejected idea is not a rejected person.

## What is not acceptable

- Harassment, in any form, in any venue connected to this project.
- Personal attacks, insults, or dismissing someone on the basis of who they
  are rather than what they said.
- Discrimination or demeaning comments relating to age, disability, ethnicity,
  gender identity or expression, nationality, personal appearance, race,
  religion, sex, sexual orientation, or socio-economic status.
- Publishing anyone's private information without their permission.
- Sustained disruption: derailing threads, reopening settled decisions
  indefinitely, or demanding unpaid attention.
- Sexualised language or imagery, and unwelcome sexual attention.

## Security-specific expectations

This is a security project, which brings a few particular obligations:

**Report vulnerabilities privately first.** [SECURITY.md](SECURITY.md) has the
process. Dropping an unreported vulnerability into a public issue puts every
person running this software at risk to make a point.

**Do not test against systems you do not own.** The [lab](labs/README.md)
exists so you never need to. It is entirely offline and uses reserved domains
specifically so demonstrating a detection does not require touching anyone
else's infrastructure.

**Do not submit malicious code**, obfuscated contributions, or dependencies
with hidden behaviour. This should not need saying, and the fact that it does
is why it is written down.

**Reporting a flaw is never treated as an attack.** Nobody will be threatened,
blamed, or spoken to unkindly for finding a problem in good faith — including
one that is embarrassing, and including one in something recently described as
finished.

## Scope

This applies in every space connected to the project — issues, pull requests,
discussions, commit messages, code comments — and when someone is representing
the project elsewhere.

## Reporting a problem

Email **james@consulting.jameshoulder.com**, or use GitHub's private reporting.

Reports go to the maintainer, who is also the person most likely to be involved
in whatever prompted the report. That is an obvious weakness of a single-person
project and there is no honest way around it. If the problem *is* the
maintainer, GitHub's [Community Guidelines reporting][gh-report] is the escalation
path that does not run through him.

Reports will be handled discreetly. The reporter's identity will not be shared
without their agreement.

## Enforcement

Proportionate to what happened:

1. **A private word.** Most things end here.
2. **A public correction**, where the behaviour was public and the record needs
   it.
3. **A temporary block** from project spaces.
4. **A permanent block.**

Serious cases — harassment, threats, deliberately malicious contributions — skip
straight to the end.
